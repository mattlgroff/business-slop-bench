# Executive Readout: Support Ticket Pilot

## Recommendation
**Do not scale now.** Authorize a matched-ticket follow-up instead.

## What the Results Show

| Measure | Baseline | Pilot | Threshold |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tickets | 200 | 100 | — |
| Mean handling time | 15 min | 12 min | — |
| Quality pass rate | 94% | 89% | ≥92% |
| Cash savings | — | Not measured | — |

The pilot handled tickets 3 minutes faster on average — a directionally encouraging signal, and the primary reason to keep this work alive.

## What the Results Do Not Support

**The speed gain is not yet attributable to the pilot.** Ticket mix differed between groups and allocation was not randomized, so the 3-minute difference could reflect an easier pilot caseload rather than a better process. The comparison cannot isolate the intervention's effect.

**Quality moved the wrong way and missed the bar.** Pass rate fell from 94% to 89%, five points below the 92% scaling threshold. This is a stated gate, not a judgment call — it is not met.

**No financial case exists.** No staffing reductions or cash savings have been measured, so faster handling has not been shown to convert into cost benefit.

Scaling on this evidence would propagate a quality decline that fails a pre-set threshold, in exchange for a time gain we cannot confirm is real or valuable.

## The Decision in Front of You

**COO: authorize Sam's matched-ticket follow-up?**

A matched design addresses the central weakness — it controls for ticket mix, allowing the handling-time difference to be interpreted as an effect of the pilot rather than of caseload.

That study should be scoped to answer three questions before any scaling decision returns:
1. Does the handling-time gain survive matched comparison?
2. Can quality reach the 92% threshold?
3. What measurable cost benefit, if any, follows from faster handling?

Recommended next step: approve the follow-up; revisit scaling once those three answers are in hand.
